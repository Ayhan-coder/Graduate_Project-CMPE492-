\begin{abstract}
Personalized instruction can improve learning outcomes, but it is hard to scale for spoken lecture and video content: the material must first be understood before it can be adapted to a learner. This paper reports a two-layer experimental evaluation of off-the-shelf automatic speech recognition (ASR) and large language model (LLM) components for making multilingual educational speech more accessible to beginner-level users. Rather than proposing a new architecture, we benchmark how these components behave on Turkish and English educational video. In the first layer, four ASR systems are compared on transcription accuracy (word and character error rates) and computational efficiency (latency, real-time factor). In the second layer, the most accurate transcripts are processed by two LLMs that extract domain-specific terminology and generate context-aware, CEFR-B1 explanations. Semantic outputs are scored with context-consistency and explanation-accuracy labels in both languages, and with a CEFR difficulty ratio and BLEU on English only. Whisper-based models produce substantially more reliable out-of-the-box transcripts than the English-oriented Wav2Vec~2.0 and SpeechBrain checkpoints used here. GPT-4.1 yields higher terminology coverage and explanation accuracy than GPT-4o-mini, at a modest latency cost. We formalize the evaluation protocol---a Turkish/English ASR split, a same-LLM cross-layer ablation, a two-annotator agreement procedure, and a dataset card---and pin a 2026 Hugging Face refresh (Whisper Large-v3 Turbo, Qwen3-ASR, and two open-weight LLMs) that officially covers Turkish and English. The language split, the ablation, the second-annotator $\kappa$, and the 2026 scores are not filled until the accompanying scripts have been run on the same seven videos. The completed pilot ran the LLMs on the best transcripts only.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Educational content is increasingly delivered as audio and video. Lectures and instructional clips are typically prepared as if every listener shares a similar level of prior knowledge. That assumption limits comprehensibility for beginner-level users, especially in passages that carry domain-specific terminology~\cite{conde2016,xu2024}.

One-to-one tutoring with mastery learning can raise average performance by roughly two standard deviations over conventional classroom instruction~\cite{bloom1984}, but human tutoring does not scale. Intelligent tutoring systems, adaptive hypermedia, and knowledge-tracing models approximate individualization at scale~\cite{brusilovsky2007,corbett1995,vanlehn2011}; large language models have more recently been used as a flexible substrate for instructional support and content generation~\cite{xu2024,vanzo2025}. Across these lines of work, a learning-support pipeline has three stages: (i)~understand the source content, (ii)~model the learner, and (iii)~adapt the delivery. For spoken lecture and video material the pipeline cannot start at (ii) or (iii). The content is locked in the audio signal, so the first stage must convert speech to text and then provide term-level semantic support. \emph{That first stage is the focus of this study.}

Manual captioning and expert correction of lecture audio do not scale~\cite{southwell2022}. Transformer ASR trained on large multilingual collections, notably Whisper~\cite{radford2023}, is more robust under noise, accent, and domain shift; self-supervised encoders such as wav2vec~2.0~\cite{baevski2020} target low-resource labeled settings; toolkits such as SpeechBrain~\cite{ravanelli2021} make controlled comparison practical. Transcription alone is not enough. Beginners struggle with the domain terms---including multi-word terms---that lectures assume~\cite{conde2016,frantzi2000}, which motivates automatic terminology extraction and beginner-level explanation~\cite{tran2026}. In the literature, ASR quality and LLM-based semantic quality are usually reported separately. Studies that evaluate both layers on the same multilingual educational material, and that include a lower-resource language such as Turkish alongside English, remain scarce.

This paper therefore asks three questions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1.} How do common off-the-shelf ASR systems compare on Turkish and English educational speech, both pooled and per language, in accuracy (WER, CER) and efficiency (latency, real-time factor)?
\item \textbf{RQ2.} Given the most accurate transcripts, how do a high-capacity LLM and a lightweight LLM compare on terminology coverage, context consistency, explanation accuracy, and---for English---beginner suitability?
\item \textbf{RQ3.} Which ASR+LLM pairings are preferable when the deployment constraint is accuracy versus when it is latency?
\end{itemize}

The completed pilot still reports pooled ASR numbers and runs both LLMs on the best transcripts. We do \emph{not} treat ASR$\to$LLM error propagation as a measured result. The accompanying repository defines the per-language table, the same-LLM ablation, and the two-annotator $\kappa$ procedure; those cells stay empty until the seven videos are re-scored.

Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item A zero-shot comparison of ASR systems on multilingual (TR/EN) educational speech, reporting raw and normalized WER/CER together with latency and real-time factor, with a required Turkish/English split (Table~\ref{tab:asr-lang}). The completed pilot uses Whisper Medium/Large-v3, Wav2Vec~2.0, and SpeechBrain; the 2026 refresh adds Whisper Large-v3 Turbo, Qwen3-ASR (1.7B and 0.6B), and a Turkish-matched Whisper Turbo fine-tune.
\item A comparison of GPT-4.1 and GPT-4o-mini on the same Whisper transcripts for terminology extraction and CEFR-B1 explanation generation, extended in the refresh by two public Hugging Face checkpoints (Qwen3-8B and Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct), plus a same-LLM ablation protocol on every ASR hypothesis (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).
\item A formal evaluation protocol (which metrics apply to which language; a two-annotator $\kappa$ procedure; a dataset card), decoding and hardware notes (Appendix~\ref{app:hw}), recommended pipeline pairings, and an evaluation kit that writes the pending tables from JSONL runs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

Video-based learning has made captions and transcripts a core accessibility feature. Automatic caption quality varies with domain terminology, speaker characteristics, and language or accent~\cite{kuhn2024}. Lecture-recording studies show that a single pooled error rate is not a sufficient report of ASR behavior~\cite{kuhn2024}, and ASR errors are known to propagate into downstream NLP tasks~\cite{southwell2022}. For Turkish, model choice is already known to matter~\cite{mercan2023}. Whisper remains a strong multilingual baseline~\cite{radford2023}; Large-v3 Turbo keeps the same $\approx$99-language coverage, including Turkish, with a pruned decoder~\cite{whisper-turbo}. Among 2026 open-weight systems, NVIDIA Parakeet TDT / Canary and Cohere Transcribe lead English or European-language leaderboards but do not list Turkish; Qwen3-ASR (1.7B and 0.6B) does, with official Turkish and English support~\cite{qwen3asr2026}. wav2vec~2.0 offers self-supervised representations under limited labels~\cite{baevski2020}; SpeechBrain provides a common experimental harness~\cite{ravanelli2021}. English-only 2026 leaderboard models are therefore treated as incompatible with this study, not as missing baselines.

Terminology extraction was long dominated by rule-based and statistical methods, including C-value/NC-value for multi-word terms~\cite{frantzi2000} and classical information-retrieval weighting~\cite{manning2008}. Deep learning then became the default in specialized domains such as biomedicine~\cite{lossio2016}. LiTeWi combines unsupervised extractors with entity linking to build educational ontologies from textbooks~\cite{conde2016}. Recent surveys emphasize transformer-based and generative approaches to automatic term extraction~\cite{tran2026}. Almost all of this work assumes clean text.

A separate literature targets the later stages of individualization---learner modeling and adapted delivery---including intelligent tutoring and adaptive hypermedia~\cite{brusilovsky2007,corbett1995,vanlehn2011} and, more recently, LLM tutors with carefully designed prompts~\cite{xu2024,vanzo2025}. Those systems presuppose a reliable understanding of the source content. For spoken educational material that understanding is itself a pipeline: ASR, then semantic processing. Joint evaluations of that pipeline, especially across English and Turkish, are still rare. This study targets that gap as a two-layer benchmark, not as a new adaptive tutor.

\section{Method}

\subsection{System Architecture}

The system under evaluation has two stages (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). Audio is first transcribed by an ASR model. The resulting text is the only input to the second stage, in which an LLM extracts domain terms and writes a short beginner-level explanation for each term. The completed pilot evaluates the stages separately and runs the LLM layer on the most accurate transcripts only. The ablation protocol feeds \emph{every} ASR hypothesis to the \emph{same} LLM (GPT-4o-mini by default) so that term count and, once labeled, R1\%/D1\% can be plotted against ASR WER.

\subsection{Semantic Analysis Layer}

The most accurate transcripts are passed to the semantic layer, with no local training or fine-tuning. The completed pilot uses two hosted models through the OpenAI API~\cite{openai2026}: GPT-4o-mini (low-latency baseline) and GPT-4.1 (higher-capacity reference). The 2026 refresh adds two public, ungated Hugging Face checkpoints so the semantic layer is no longer API-only: \texttt{Qwen/Qwen3-8B} (open-weight capacity) and \texttt{Qwen/Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct} (open-weight latency). Aya Expanse 8B lists Turkish but is gated and non-commercial, so it is not in the primary set.

The instruction is held fixed across all runs. The model must (i)~extract domain-specific terms, including multi-word terms, as a JSON list, and (ii)~write one short CEFR-B1 explanation per term that uses only information consistent with the transcript. The full template is given in Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}. Temperature and other generation settings are left at API defaults and are not varied.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

\subsubsection{ASR layer}
We report word error rate (WER) and character error rate (CER) against the reference transcripts~\cite{jurafsky2026}. For a reference of $N$ words,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{WER}=\frac{S+D+I}{N},
\label{eq:wer}
\end{equation}
where $S$, $D$, and $I$ are substitutions, deletions, and insertions. CER is defined analogously over $N_c$ reference characters. Both metrics are computed on raw output and on a normalized form (lowercasing, punctuation removal, whitespace cleanup) so that formatting differences are not counted as content errors. CER is the more stable of the two under the morphological richness of Turkish.

Efficiency is reported as wall-clock latency and as the real-time factor
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RTF}=\frac{t_{\mathrm{proc}}}{t_{\mathrm{audio}}},
\label{eq:rtf}
\end{equation}
so that $\mathrm{RTF}<1$ means faster-than-real-time processing.

\subsubsection{Semantic layer}
Outputs are scored against dictionary references: the Turkish Language Association (TDK) dictionary for Turkish~\cite{tdk2026} and the Oxford English Dictionary for English~\cite{oed2026}. Two binary labels are assigned by a domain-knowledgeable annotator. The completed pilot used a single annotator. The repository includes a two-annotator protocol and a Cohen's $\kappa$ script; $\kappa$ is reported only after a second annotator has labeled a subset independently (Appendix~\ref{app:iaa}):

\begin{itemize}
\item $R$: the extracted term is consistent with the transcript context ($R1$) or not ($R0$);
\item $D$: the generated explanation is correct relative to the dictionary ($D1$) or not ($D0$).
\end{itemize}

From these counts we report
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{R1\%}=\frac{R1}{R1+R0},\qquad
\mathrm{D1\%}=\frac{D1}{D1+D0}.
\label{eq:rd}
\end{equation}
R1\% is a precision-like score over emitted terms; there is no gold term list, so recall is not reported. Term count is therefore presented alongside R1\%, not as a coverage claim by itself.

For English only we also report a CEFR-based B1+ ratio and BLEU~\cite{papineni2002}. The B1+ ratio is the fraction of explanation tokens at CEFR level B1 or above,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{B1^{+}\ ratio}=\frac{\lvert\{w:\mathrm{CEFR}(w)\ge\mathrm{B1}\}\rvert}{\lvert\{w\}\rvert},
\label{eq:b1}
\end{equation}
so a \emph{lower} value is more beginner-friendly. These two metrics are English-only because there is no equally standardized CEFR word list for Turkish and because BLEU requires aligned reference definitions. Table~\ref{tab:protocol} states which metric applies to which language.

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Reproducibility}

Pilot checkpoints: Whisper \texttt{medium} / \texttt{large-v3}; Wav2Vec~2.0 \texttt{facebook/wav2vec2-large-xlsr-53}; SpeechBrain with default greedy/beam settings; GPT-4.1 and GPT-4o-mini via the OpenAI API~\cite{openai2026}. Refresh checkpoints and pinned revisions are listed in Table~\ref{tab:hf2026} and in \texttt{experiments/models.json}. The refresh scripts force \texttt{task=transcribe}, set \texttt{language} from the clip when known, decode long-form audio in 30\,s chunks, and leave \texttt{condition\_on\_previous\_text} off by default. R/D labels in the pilot were assigned by a single annotator. Exact prompt wording is in Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}.

On the pooled numbers in Table~\ref{tab:asr} and Fig.~\ref{fig:wer}, the two Whisper models have much lower error than Wav2Vec~2.0 and SpeechBrain. Normalized WER/CER are the figures we use when discussing downstream usability. Table~\ref{tab:rtf} shows the complementary efficiency picture: Wav2Vec~2.0 is the cheapest (mean RTF~$0.051$) and SpeechBrain the most expensive (mean RTF~$2.005$, slower than real time). Whisper Medium sits in a usable range (mean RTF~$0.156$) at the lowest error.

Two points of interpretation matter. First, Whisper is pretrained on large-scale multilingual data, whereas the Wav2Vec~2.0 and SpeechBrain checkpoints used here are primarily English-oriented. The high error of the latter two, especially on the Turkish portion of the set, is an out-of-the-box coverage result. We therefore rank systems by suitability for this multilingual setting, not by architecture. The 2026 refresh replaces that gap with Qwen3-ASR and a Turkish-matched Whisper Turbo fine-tune (Table~\ref{tab:hf2026}) rather than fine-tuning Wav2Vec~2.0 or SpeechBrain.

Second, Whisper Medium has a \emph{much} lower normalized WER than Large-v3 ($0.021$ vs.\ $0.129$ --- about six times lower), not a slight edge. This is consistent with Large-v3's known sensitivity, on long-form audio, to language detection and to decoding flags such as \texttt{condition\_on\_previous\_text}. We treat the gap as configuration-dependent and do not present Medium as generally superior to Large-v3.

Semantic results are in Table~\ref{tab:sem}. GPT-4.1 emits more terms than GPT-4o-mini in both languages (105 vs.\ 56 on English; 78 vs.\ 36 on Turkish) while keeping R1\% and D1\% high (96.2\% context consistency in both languages; explanation accuracy 99.0\% EN / 98.7\% TR). \section{Limitations}

This is a focused benchmark. (i)~Dataset size: seven videos, about 95 minutes; reported values are averages over this set, with no significance tests. (ii)~ASR scope of the completed pilot: default checkpoints only, so that ranking is out-of-the-box multilingual suitability, not each architecture's ceiling. (iii)~Tables~\ref{tab:asr-lang}, \ref{tab:asr-2026}, and \ref{tab:ablation} are part of the protocol but are empty until the evaluation kit is run on the original wavs; we do not invent those cells. (iv)~Semantic labels in the pilot: a single annotator; $\kappa$ is defined but not yet measured. R/D are binary and there is no gold term list, so recall is unmeasured. (v)~Language coverage of metrics: B1+ and BLEU are English-only. (vi)~API-hosted LLMs: outputs can change as providers update models. (vii)~Pilot LLMs were run on the best transcripts only. (viii)~Dataset card fields for source, license, and speakers are unknown until the authors complete them.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

We presented a two-layer evaluation of off-the-shelf ASR and LLM components for beginner-oriented support on Turkish and English educational speech. Whisper-based models, especially Medium under our decoding setup, provided usable transcripts at acceptable real-time factor. GPT-4.1 extracted more terms than GPT-4o-mini and produced more beginner-friendly English explanations, at higher and more variable latency. For accuracy-first deployments we recommend Whisper Medium with GPT-4.1; for latency-first deployments, Whisper Medium with GPT-4o-mini.

We did not measure how ASR errors propagate into LLM outputs. Immediate next step is to drop the seven wavs into the evaluation kit and fill Tables~\ref{tab:asr-lang}, \ref{tab:asr-2026}, and \ref{tab:ablation}, then double-label a subset for $\kappa$. Further work: (a)~intermediate modules such as confidence filtering and ASR-aware prompting; (b)~prompt, chain-of-thought, and retrieval-augmented variants of the term-extraction instruction; (c)~a larger set, statistical tests, and symmetric metrics for both languages. A longer-term goal is to wrap this first stage in an adaptive loop that shows a learner only the terms they still need.

\section{Prompt Template}

The following instruction was held fixed across all runs. Placeholders \texttt{\{LANG\}} and \texttt{\{TRANSCRIPT\}} are filled at call time.

\begin{quote}

You are a terminology assistant for \{\texttt{LANG}\} educational content. Step 1: From the transcript below, extract domain-specific terms (including multi-word terms). \section{Decoding Settings and Hardware}
\label{app:hw}

Two settings must not be mixed. \emph{Pilot (Tables~\ref{tab:asr}--\ref{tab:rtf}).} All four ASR systems were run on CPU with default pretrained weights. The original draft did not record the CPU model, Whisper \texttt{language} / \texttt{condition\_on\_previous\_text}, beam size, or chunk length. Those published scores, including the Medium vs.\ Large-v3 gap, are therefore configuration-sensitive.

\emph{Refresh kit.} Scripts force \texttt{task=transcribe} and, when the clip language is known, \texttt{language=tr} or \texttt{en}. Long-form audio is decoded in 30\,s chunks with timestamps. \texttt{condition\_on\_previous\_text} is off unless \texttt{--condition-on-prev} is set. Device is CUDA if a GPU build of PyTorch is available, otherwise CPU. Each run writes a sidecar metadata file with these flags.

\emph{Kit development machine (28 August 2026), not claimed as the pilot host.} Intel Core i7-14650HX (16 cores / 24 threads), 32\,GB RAM, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5060 Laptop GPU, Windows, Python 3.12. The PyTorch install present when the kit was written was CPU-only (2.12.0+cpu). Qwen3-ASR 1.7B and Qwen3-8B are not practical on that CPU wheel.

\section{Annotation and Agreement}
\label{app:iaa}

R and D are binary (Section~III-E). Annotators work independently from the same item list, using TDK or OED, and do not see each other's labels. Cohen's $\kappa$~\cite{cohen1960} is computed separately for R and for D on the double-labeled subset; adjudication, if any, is used only for the final R1/D1 counts, not for $\kappa$. The item schema and the scoring script are in the accompanying repository (\texttt{annotation/PROTOCOL.md}, \texttt{iaa.py}). This draft does not invent a $\kappa$ value.
