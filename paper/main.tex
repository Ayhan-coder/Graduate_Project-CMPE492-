\begin{abstract}
Personalized instruction significantly improves learning outcomes, but scaling individualized pedagogical support to spoken educational video requires a robust pipeline that first accurately transcribes audio and then extracts domain concepts for learner adaptation. This paper presents a comprehensive, three-layer empirical benchmark evaluating automatic speech recognition (ASR), large language model (LLM), and generative video-animation components on Turkish and English educational lectures. All measured results rest on a $7$-lecture gold set ($\approx$95.6 min; 3 Turkish, 4 English) with human-verified reference transcripts and timestamped terminology; a curated $32$-lecture candidate manifest (\texttt{manifest\_30plus.jsonl}, $\approx$7.28\,h) overlaps the $7$ gold clips, includes $19$ raw disk captures ($18$ of which fail duration verification against manifest timestamps), and contains no evaluated extension clips. In the speech layer, we benchmark modern multilingual checkpoints (Whisper Medium/Large-v3/Turbo, Qwen3-ASR, Nemotron~3.5, and Turkish fine-tuned models) and \emph{measure} three acoustic interventions --- energy-based Voice Activity Detection (VAD) chunking, terminology domain biasing and LLM-based post-correction --- against one seven-clip baseline; the VAD chunker turns WER \emph{upward} on every clip (Turkish macro $0.021 \rightarrow 0.041$, English $0.010 \rightarrow 0.026$) rather than removing the silence-hallucination loops, which are absent from the GPU baseline ($8$-gram repetition $0.0122$/$0.0000$), so it ships disabled. In the semantic layer, we separate two analyses that are easy to conflate: terminology extraction over eleven \emph{measured} ASR hypotheses scored against closed gold lists, and a controlled-WER noise ladder ($0\%$--$40\%$) that we report as an \emph{analytical degradation model} rather than a measurement, because it samples the gold list directly instead of invoking the language model. The gap between the two is the substantive finding: the model predicts $F_1 \approx 0.97$ at $2\%$ WER where measured extraction reaches $0.364$ (Turkish) and $0.679$ (English), so extraction under-recall dominates acoustic corruption. Furthermore, we audit our own prompting-strategy comparison and find it circular: the retrieval arm was fed the very gold term list used for scoring, and the previously reported $47.6/60.9/76.9\%$ $F_1$ triple is reproduced exactly by the fixture path, which forces precision to $1.0$ and samples the first 10/14/20 gold terms --- so those strategy gains and their $3.42$--$6.85$\,s latencies are withdrawn. Re-running the two non-leaking arms on the local checkpoint on one clip per language gives CoT over Zero-Shot $F_1$ of $51.2\%$ against $40.0\%$ on TR-1, and a parsability failure ($F_1 = 0$) against $64.0\%$ on EN-1, at $71$--$81$\,s per call; we report this as a probe, not as a benchmark. In addition to local 4-bit edge execution, we benchmark sovereign cloud infrastructure (Türkiye SSB/TÜBİTAK EVREN National AI Platform, evaluating Qwen~3.8 Flash and DeepSeek~V4.1 Flash on H200 GPU clusters). The measured latency advantage is model-specific rather than platform-wide: Qwen~3.8 Flash reaches $6.11$\,s on Turkish, $6.5\times$ faster than the local 4-bit run on the same clips, while DeepSeek~V4.1 Flash at $27.68$\,s is slower than both commercial APIs yet extracts twice the terminology. We report inter-annotator reliability for the binary rubric on the $115$ double-labelled pairs ($\kappa_R = 0.782$, $\mathrm{PABAK}_R = 0.930$; $\kappa_D = 1.000$ over $P_e = 0.949$, the prevalence paradox in its clearest form) and use an automated local-LLM judge only where its per-item output is stored---a previously claimed judge-versus-human validation is withdrawn because no judge predictions were persisted. Measured readability ($807$ explanations scored with Ate\c{s}man for Turkish and Flesch for English) places only $18$--$45\%$ of individual explanations inside the target B1 band, and reference-definition similarity is reported as a \emph{lexical proxy} rather than contextual-embedding BERTScore because the optional \texttt{bert\_score} package is absent from the experiment environment. An enriched downstream educational schema featuring Bloom taxonomy levels and concept prerequisite directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) completes the pipeline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Educational content is increasingly delivered as audio and video. Lectures and instructional clips are typically prepared as if every listener shares a similar level of prior knowledge. That assumption severely limits comprehensibility for beginner-level users, especially in passages that carry dense domain-specific terminology~\cite{conde2016,xu2024}.

One-to-one tutoring with mastery learning can raise average student performance by roughly two standard deviations over conventional classroom instruction~\cite{bloom1984}, but human tutoring does not scale. Intelligent tutoring systems, adaptive hypermedia, and knowledge-tracing models approximate individualization at scale~\cite{brusilovsky2007,corbett1995,vanlehn2011}; large language models have more recently been used as a flexible substrate for instructional support and content generation~\cite{xu2024,vanzo2025}. Across these lines of work, a learning-support pipeline operates in three stages: (i)~understand the source content, (ii)~model the learner, and (iii)~adapt the delivery. For spoken lecture and video material, the pipeline cannot begin at (ii) or (iii) because the content is locked in the audio signal. The initial module must therefore convert speech to text and subsequently extract domain terminology to provide beginner-level scaffolding. \emph{That content-understanding module is the focus of this study.}

While manual captioning and expert curation of lecture audio do not scale~\cite{southwell2022}, transformer ASR trained on large multilingual collections, notably Whisper~\cite{radford2023}, exhibits substantial resilience under acoustic noise and domain shift. Nevertheless, transcription alone is insufficient: novice learners struggle with technical jargon, motivating automatic terminology extraction, pedagogical simplification, and structural concept mapping~\cite{tran2026,conde2016}. In the literature, ASR transcription accuracy and LLM semantic capabilities are predominantly reported in isolation. Rigorous evaluations that directly measure how ASR degradation propagates into downstream LLM extraction, especially across lower-resource languages such as Turkish alongside English, remain scarce.

This paper addresses this gap by investigating four central research questions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1 (ASR Performance \& Acoustic Enhancements).} How do modern open-weight and commercial ASR architectures compare on Turkish and English educational speech in transcription accuracy (WER, CER) and efficiency (latency, RTF), and do long-form VAD chunking, terminology domain biasing and LLM post-correction improve those metrics?
\item \textbf{RQ2 (Cross-Layer Degradation).} How far does downstream terminology extraction $F_1$ actually fall as upstream transcription quality degrades, and how much of the published degradation profile is measured rather than modelled? This paper answers by separating measured ASR$\to$LLM points from an analytical noise-injection ladder, and reports the gap between them.
\item \textbf{RQ3 (Prompting Strategies \& Semantic Quality).} How do structured prompting strategies (Zero-Shot, Chain-of-Thought, and Glossary-based RAG) affect terminology recall, precision, and beginner suitability across different LLM parameter scales? This paper answers it partially: we re-measure the two arms that can be scored without leakage on one clip per language, and we show that the third arm cannot be evaluated against the glossary it retrieves from.
\item \textbf{RQ4 (Evaluation Reliability \& Downstream Integration).} Can an automated LLM-as-a-judge reliably surrogate human annotator decisions ($R$ and $D$), how does class prevalence impact agreement metrics ($\kappa$ vs.\ PABAK), and how can extracted concepts be structured into prerequisite DAGs for downstream adaptive hypermedia?
\end{itemize}

Our principal contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Gold-Standard Multilingual Dataset (7 Evaluated Clips):} All measured results rest on $7$ lectures ($\approx$95.6 min; 3 Turkish, 4 English) with human-verified reference transcripts (\texttt{experiments/data/gold/}). A separate curation artefact, \texttt{manifest\_30plus.jsonl}, enumerates $32$ candidate lectures ($16$ Turkish, $16$ English, $7.28$\,h) with retrieved caption text. This manifest overlaps the $7$ gold clips; while $19$ candidate audio captures exist on disk, only EN-11 matches duration metadata while $18$ remain unverified raw captures pending slice trimming, and no new extension clip was evaluated. It is reported as future-work scope, not as an evaluated extension corpus. Closed gold terminology lists cover only TR-1 and EN-1 ($32$ terms each).
\item \textbf{Cross-Layer Analysis, Separated into Measurement and Model:} We report terminology $F_1$ for eleven \emph{measured} ASR hypotheses passed through a fixed LLM, and we report the controlled-WER ladder ($0\%$--$40\%$) explicitly as an \emph{analytical degradation model} that samples the gold list rather than invoking the model. The two series disagree by a wide margin ($F_1$ of $0.364$ measured versus $\approx0.97$ modelled at $2\%$ WER), which is itself the finding: extraction under-recall, not acoustic corruption, is the binding constraint.
\item \textbf{ASR Optimization Suite, Measured Rather Than Asserted:} We implement energy-based VAD chunking, slide/glossary-based terminology biasing (\texttt{processor.get\_prompt\_ids}) and LLM-based ASR post-correction, and run all three against the same seven-clip Large-v3 baseline (Sec.~\ref{sec:acoustic-opt}, Table~\ref{tab:interventions}). The measurement reverses two claims of an earlier draft: the energy VAD chunker \emph{raises} normalised WER on every clip (TR macro $0.021 \rightarrow 0.041$, EN $0.010 \rightarrow 0.026$) instead of eliminating repetition loops, because the GPU baseline contains no measurable loops ($8$-gram repetition rate $0.0122$ TR / $0.0000$ EN), and the biasing arm as previously published was not decodable at all --- the glossary prompt plus $440$ new tokens overflowed Whisper's $448$-position decoder budget and made \texttt{generate()} raise, which is why no '+Domain Biasing' artifact existed. Turkish fine-tuned checkpoints are also benchmarked.
\item \textbf{Structured Prompting, Sovereign Cloud, \& Rating Provenance:} We audit the prompting-strategy comparison rather than report it, showing that its retrieval arm scored against the gold list it was given; we benchmark national sovereign inference (EVREN platform) where the latency gain is model-specific ($6.11$\,s for Qwen 3.8 Flash against $27.68$\,s for DeepSeek V4.1, the latter slower than both commercial APIs); and we make the source of every $R$/$D$ rating explicit --- human annotator for the six Whisper cells, stored per-item judge output for the 2026 checkpoints and EVREN cells, and no claim where neither exists --- after withdrawing a judge-versus-human validation whose predictions were never persisted.
\item \textbf{Symmetric Readability \& Enriched Downstream Schema:} We implement symmetric readability evaluation (Ate\c{s}man for Turkish, Flesch for English), reported as the \emph{share of individual explanations} falling inside the target band rather than as a band-level average, plus a reference-definition similarity that runs as a \emph{lexical proxy} because the optional \texttt{bert\_score} package is absent from the experiment environment, and establish an enriched output schema (v2.0) defining Bloom taxonomy levels, multi-tier pedagogical explanations (B1 analogy, formal definition, elevator pitch, exam tips), and concept prerequisite DAGs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

Video-based learning has made captions and transcripts a core accessibility feature. Automatic caption quality varies with domain terminology, speaker characteristics, and language or accent~\cite{kuhn2024}. Lecture-recording studies show that a single pooled error rate is not a sufficient report of ASR behavior~\cite{kuhn2024}, and ASR errors are known to propagate into downstream NLP tasks~\cite{southwell2022}. For Turkish, model choice is already known to matter~\cite{mercan2023}. Whisper remains a strong multilingual baseline~\cite{radford2023}; Large-v3 Turbo keeps the same $\approx$99-language coverage, including Turkish, with a pruned decoder~\cite{whisper-turbo}. Among 2026 open-weight systems, NVIDIA Parakeet TDT / Canary and Cohere Transcribe lead English or European-language leaderboards but do not list Turkish; Qwen3-ASR (1.7B and 0.6B) does, with official Turkish and English support~\cite{qwen3asr2026}. NVIDIA Nemotron~3.5 ASR (0.6B) is the later NVIDIA checkpoint that \emph{does} list Turkish (\texttt{tr-TR}) and English as transcription-ready locales~\cite{nemotron35asr2026}. wav2vec~2.0 offers self-supervised representations under limited labels~\cite{baevski2020}; SpeechBrain provides a common experimental harness~\cite{ravanelli2021}. English-only 2026 leaderboard models are therefore treated as incompatible with this study, not as missing baselines. Alongside global models, sovereign AI initiatives such as the EVREN National AI Platform~\cite{evren2026} provide dedicated H200 inference clusters for Turkish and multilingual models, ensuring strict data residency while eliminating client GPU resource ceilings.

Terminology extraction was long dominated by rule-based and statistical methods, including C-value/NC-value for multi-word terms~\cite{frantzi2000} and classical information-retrieval weighting~\cite{manning2008}. Deep learning then became the default in specialized domains such as biomedicine~\cite{lossio2016}. LiTeWi combines unsupervised extractors with entity linking to build educational ontologies from textbooks~\cite{conde2016}. Recent surveys emphasize transformer-based and generative approaches to automatic term extraction~\cite{tran2026}. Almost all of this work assumes clean text.

A separate literature targets the later stages of individualization---learner modeling and adapted delivery---including intelligent tutoring and adaptive hypermedia~\cite{brusilovsky2007,corbett1995,vanlehn2011} and, more recently, LLM tutors with carefully designed prompts~\cite{xu2024,vanzo2025}. Those systems presuppose a reliable understanding of the source content. For spoken educational material that understanding is itself a pipeline: ASR, then semantic processing. Joint evaluations of that pipeline, especially across English and Turkish, are still rare. This study targets that gap as a three-layer pipeline: the speech and semantic layers are benchmarked quantitatively, whereas the third (video-generation) layer is implemented and demonstrated end-to-end but is not scored against a gold reference. It is not a new adaptive tutor.

\section{Method}

\subsection{System Architecture}

The evaluation framework comprises an integrated three-layer pipeline enhanced with acoustic and semantic scaffolding (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). In the speech recognition layer, the delivered application slices the normalised audio into fixed, non-overlapping $30$\,s windows (\texttt{gui/pipeline.py}, \texttt{CHUNK\_S = 30.0}) and transcribes them sequentially, while the benchmark harness runs Whisper over the whole track in \texttt{sequential\_generate} mode; the energy-based VAD chunker, the terminology-prompt conditioning (\texttt{processor.get\_prompt\_ids}) and the LLM-based post-correction pass are therefore experiment-only switches (\texttt{run\_asr.py --use-vad}, \texttt{asr\_post\_correction.py}) evaluated in Sec.~\ref{sec:acoustic-opt}, not part of the default transcription path. In the semantic analysis layer, the transcribed text is processed by an LLM guided by structured prompting strategies (Zero-Shot, Chain-of-Thought, or Glossary-based RAG) to extract domain terminology, evaluate context consistency ($R$) and explanation accuracy ($D$), and structure outputs into an enriched educational schema featuring Bloom taxonomy levels, multi-tier pedagogical explanations, and concept prerequisite directed acyclic graphs (DAGs). In the delivery layer, the learner marks a moment of confusion in the synchronized player; the transcript window $[t-45\,\text{s},\,t+10\,\text{s}]$ and the term nearest to $t$ are passed to the local \texttt{Qwen3.5-4B} model, which returns a narration script plus Manim scene code. That code is checked against an AST allow-list (only \texttt{from manim import *} may be imported), rendered at 480p15 (the initial draft tier; the seven shipped video artifacts predate the introduction of configurable 720p30/1080p30 tiers in the delivery UI) with up to two automatic repair attempts, voiced by Edge-TTS, and multiplexed with FFmpeg into a downloadable MP4.

\subsection{Speech Recognition Layer and Enhancements}

We evaluate eight off-the-shelf and fine-tuned ASR systems (Table~\ref{tab:hf2026}): Whisper Medium, Large-v3, and Large-v3 Turbo~\cite{radford2023,whisper-turbo}; Qwen3-ASR (1.7B and 0.6B)~\cite{qwen3asr2026}; NVIDIA Nemotron~3.5 ASR (0.6B)~\cite{nemotron35asr2026}; and domain/language-matched checkpoints (\texttt{selimc/whisper-large-v3-turbo-turkish} and \texttt{mpoyraz/wav2vec2-xls-r-300m-cv7-turkish}~\cite{mercan2023}).

Three critical acoustic enhancements are introduced:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Energy-Based VAD Chunking, Tested and Found Harmful:} Whisper is known to enter repetitive decoding loops on continuous, unedited recordings with prolonged silences. We therefore implement an energy-based Voice Activity Detection chunker (\texttt{vad\_chunker.py}: $30$\,ms frames, cut line at $\max(10^{-4}, 2.2 \times$ the $25$th-percentile noise floor), gaps $\le 200$\,ms closed, boundaries searched on pauses $>0.4$\,s, producing $12$--$28$\,s segments) and benchmark it against the same clips without it. The intervention \emph{degrades} accuracy: normalised WER rises on all seven clips (TR macro $0.021 \rightarrow 0.041$, EN $0.010 \rightarrow 0.026$), no repetition loop existed in the GPU baseline to eliminate, and terminology coverage is unchanged. The earlier claim that it "completely eliminates hallucination loops" and cuts Turkish WER by $83.7\%$ is withdrawn; the chunker remains in the codebase as an optional flag, unused by the delivered application.
\item \textbf{Terminology Domain Biasing, Tested and Found Harmful and Leaking:} Technical lectures contain specialized proper nouns and acronyms (e.g., \emph{ANOVA, Th\'evenin, Qualia}). Using Whisper's prompt-conditioning interface (\texttt{processor.get\_prompt\_ids}), we inject the closed glossary into the decoder prefix, steering beam search toward curriculum spellings. Measured against the same stage without the prompt it multiplies the error on the two clips it touches (TR-1 $0.0333 \rightarrow 0.1562$, $4.7\times$; EN-1 $0.0194 \rightarrow 0.0869$, $4.5\times$) and is the sole cause of the stage's macro rise ($+99.4\%$ TR, $+64.4\%$ EN); on TR-1 the decoder echoes the $68$-word glossary back instead of transcribing, and the coverage it "gains" is the injected list reappearing, which is leakage against the very file the scorer reads (Sec.~\ref{sec:acoustic-opt}).
\item \textbf{LLM-Based ASR Post-Correction, Tested and Marginal:} To recover from acoustic misrecognitions before semantic parsing, transcripts pass through a targeted post-correction prompt on the local \texttt{Qwen3.5-4B} checkpoint. Applied to the stage-3 hypotheses it lowers WER on four of seven clips but leaves no clip below its own raw baseline, and it amplifies the one transcript that is already degenerate (TR-1 $0.156 \rightarrow 0.324$). Its published rescue figure is withdrawn: the "$26.3\%$ simulated WER, $+29.8\%$ $F_1$" pair comes from the harness self-test, whose corrected string is the gold reference pasted verbatim.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Semantic Analysis Layer and Prompting Strategies}
\label{sec:prompting}

To assess semantic extraction beyond fixed zero-shot queries, we formulate three distinct prompting paradigms:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Zero-Shot Baseline:} Direct extraction instruction requesting domain terms and CEFR-B1 explanations.
\item \textbf{Chain-of-Thought (CoT):} The model first explicitly identifies lecture topics and sub-domains, filters candidate multi-word expressions against generic discourse vocabulary, and then constructs beginner-friendly definitions.
\item \textbf{Glossary-Based RAG:} Retrieval-augmented generation where syllabus concepts and slide keywords are supplied as contextual anchor primitives, guiding extraction toward curriculum-aligned terminology. \emph{Implemented but not evaluated here};\footnote{The subsequent 8-clip corporate speech engineering probe ($60.7\%$ candidate grounding precision on long-form transcripts; \texttt{experiments/rag/eval\_corporate\_rag.py}) postdates this paper and is reported in the extended graduation project report §8.4.} Section~\ref{sec:prompt-strat} explains why scoring this arm would be circular without a held-out glossary.
\end{enumerate}

Extracted items are structured into an \textbf{Enriched Educational Schema (v2.0)} comprising: canonical term lemma, audio timestamp span ($[t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{end}}]$), pedagogical difficulty (\texttt{Beginner}, \texttt{Intermediate}, \texttt{Advanced}), Bloom's taxonomy cognitive level (\texttt{Remembering}, \texttt{Understanding}, \texttt{Applying}), concept prerequisite DAG edges, and four pedagogical explanation variants (\texttt{b1\_analogy}, \texttt{formal\_definition}, \texttt{elevator\_pitch}, \texttt{exam\_tip}).

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

\subsubsection{ASR layer}
We report word error rate (WER) and character error rate (CER) against reference transcripts~\cite{jurafsky2026}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{WER}=\frac{S+D+I}{N}, \qquad \mathrm{CER}=\frac{S_c+D_c+I_c}{N_c},
\label{eq:wer}
\end{equation}
computed across raw text and normalized forms (lowercasing, punctuation stripping, Turkish dotted/dotless \i{}/i folding). Efficiency is reported via real-time factor ($\mathrm{RTF} = t_{\mathrm{proc}} / t_{\mathrm{audio}}$).

\subsubsection{Semantic layer}
Outputs are scored against standardized dictionary authorities (TDK for Turkish~\cite{tdk2026}, Oxford for English~\cite{oed2026}):
\begin{itemize}
\item $R$: context consistency with the lecture ($R1$) or invalid extraction ($R0$);
\item $D$: factual and pedagogical accuracy of the definition ($D1$) or incorrect ($D0$).
\end{itemize}
From these counts we report $\mathrm{R1\%} = R1/(R1+R0)$ and $\mathrm{D1\%} = D1/(D1+D0)$. 

\subsubsection{Symmetric Readability and Semantic Similarity}
To eliminate the linguistic asymmetry of English-only readability metrics, we adopt language-tailored readability formulations:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Turkish: Ate\c{s}man Readability Index~\cite{atesman1997}:}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Score}_{\text{Ate\c{s}man}} = 198.825 - 40.175 \left(\frac{N_{\mathrm{syl}}}{N_{\mathrm{words}}}\right) - 2.610 \left(\frac{N_{\mathrm{words}}}{N_{\mathrm{sent}}}\right),
\label{eq:atesman}
\end{equation}
where $N_{\mathrm{syl}}$ is computed via vowel phoneme counting. Scores between $50$ and $69$ denote ``Orta Güçlükte'' (intermediate), matching target CEFR B1 pedagogical difficulty.
\item \textbf{English: Flesch Reading Ease:}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{Flesch}} = 206.835 - 1.015 \left(\frac{N_{\mathrm{words}}}{N_{\mathrm{sent}}}\right) - 84.6 \left(\frac{N_{\mathrm{syl}}}{N_{\mathrm{words}}}\right),
\label{eq:flesch}
\end{equation}
where scores between $60$ and $70$ indicate standard B1 readability.
\item \textbf{Semantic Similarity:} Replaces weak token-level BLEU by scoring each explanation against its gold reference definition. The intended metric is BERTScore~\cite{zhang2020bertscore} with contextualized embeddings, but the optional \texttt{bert\_score} package is not installed in the experiment environment, so every number reported in Table~\ref{tab:readability} comes from the documented fallback in \texttt{eval\_semantic.compute\_bert\_score}: a character-trigram cosine similarity blended with word-set precision and recall. We therefore call it a \emph{lexical proxy} throughout and do not interpret it as a contextual-embedding similarity.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Inter-Annotator Agreement and Prevalence}
We evaluate two-rater agreement using Cohen's $\kappa$~\cite{cohen1960} and Fleiss' $\kappa$. To resolve the well-known prevalence paradox on Metric $D$ (where $\approx 97\%$ positive prevalence deflates raw $\kappa$), we compute the Prevalence-Adjusted and Bias-Adjusted Kappa (PABAK~\cite{byrt1993}):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PABAK} = 2 P_o - 1.
\label{eq:pabak}
\end{equation}
Temporal alignment on timestamped extractions is validated using temporal Intersection over Union ($\mathrm{IoU} \ge 0.3$). Table~\ref{tab:protocol} summarizes metric applicability.

Combined with the GPU ASR benchmarks, the deployment pairings are refined as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Accuracy-oriented (Server / Offline Batch):} Whisper Large-v3 with a capacity LLM (commercial GPT-4.1 via API, or \texttt{Qwen3.5-9B}), when transcription accuracy ($0.021$ TR / $0.010$ EN) and exhaustive domain terminology recall ($46.9\%$) take precedence over runtime. The $9$B checkpoint was only ever run in our host-memory-offloaded configuration ($513.19$\,s mean over two Turkish clips), so we recommend it for batch use on the strength of that measurement rather than a sized GPU requirement.
\item \textbf{Practical Local Deployment (Laptop / Edge):} Whisper Turbo (or Whisper Large-v3) paired with \textbf{Qwen3.5-4B}, providing a complete local, zero-API pipeline: measured mean ASR RTF $0.025$--$0.044$ and a $39.55$\,s mean LLM latency on the Turkish Large-v3 feed, with strong context precision ($90.3\%$ TR / $98.0\%$ EN, human-rated). For this checkpoint we instrumented memory directly (\texttt{outputs/local\_vram\_probe.json}: $3.08$\,GB of weights resident after a 4-bit nf4 load, $4.72$\,GB peak torch allocator and $5.26$\,GB total device occupancy during a full clip call on the $8.12$\,GB RTX~5060), so the pairing is demonstrated to fit on this host. No headroom claim extends to the other checkpoints, whose peak occupancy was never instrumented.
\end{itemize}

Wav2Vec~2.0 is cheaper than Whisper but, on this set and these checkpoints, is not accurate enough to recommend as the front-end of a terminology pipeline.

\subsection{Cross-Layer ASR-to-LLM Error Propagation Analysis}
\label{sec:cross-layer}

A central claim in modular educational speech processing is that upstream ASR transcription accuracy directly governs downstream semantic concept extraction. While intuitive, previous benchmarks evaluated ASR and LLMs either in isolation or only on clean, near-zero-WER transcripts. This subsection separates two things that are easy to conflate: a set of \emph{measured} points, in which real ASR hypotheses are fed to the fixed LLM, and an \emph{analytical degradation model} sampled from the gold list at controlled WER levels.

\subsubsection{Measured points (real ASR hypotheses through the LLM)}
Table~\ref{tab:cross-layer} reports terminology $F_1$ for eleven real ASR hypotheses, each run through \texttt{Qwen3.5-4B} and scored against the closed gold list of TR-1/EN-1. Every cell is a single clip, so these are indications rather than population estimates. Two observations hold across the measured set. First, Turkish $F_1$ spans $0.238$--$0.439$ and English $F_1$ spans $0.565$--$0.679$, i.e.\ the semantic layer under-recovers gold terms in both languages even when WER is low. Second, the mapping from WER to $F_1$ is not monotone within the measured range: \texttt{Nemotron 3.5} reaches the best Turkish $F_1$ ($0.439$) from the second-worst Turkish WER ($0.117$), while \texttt{Whisper Large-v3} produces $0.364$ from a much cleaner transcript ($0.017$). Low WER is therefore necessary but not sufficient for terminology recovery; how the residual errors are \emph{distributed} matters, and a transcript can be word-accurate while still fragmenting or dropping the specific multi-word terms the gold list contains. The clearest single signal remains the high-WER end: \texttt{Qwen3-ASR 0.6B} at $0.136$ WER gives the worst Turkish $F_1$ ($0.238$).

\subsubsection{The controlled-WER ladder is an analytical model, not a measurement}
The second series in Fig.~\ref{fig:cross_layer_curve} is produced by \texttt{run\_cross\_layer\_curve.py} in its default mode, which does \emph{not} call the LLM. It draws each of the $32$ gold terms independently with survival probability $(1-\mathrm{WER})^{\max(1,\,0.8\ell)}$ (with $\ell$ the term length in words) and injects a number of spurious terms proportional to the WER. Consequently its $0\%$ anchor is $F_1 = 1.000$ \emph{by construction}, and the resulting trajectory characterises the assumed survival function, not model behaviour. Its modelled endpoints are TR $1.000 \to 0.458$ and EN $1.000 \to 0.604$ over $0\% \to 40\%$ injected error; the Turkish series is additionally non-monotone ($0.717$ at $19.3\%$ rising to $0.737$ at $29.3\%$) because each level is one Bernoulli sample, so the apparent bumps are sampling noise.

The comparison that matters is between the two series, and it is unfavourable to the model: at $\approx$2\% WER the analytical ladder predicts $F_1 \approx 0.97$, whereas the measured Whisper Large-v3 point is $0.364$ (TR) and $0.679$ (EN). The simulation's optimism comes from its premise --- it assumes the LLM is handed the correct term inventory and only loses entries to corruption, and it holds extraction recall at the gold level. Real extraction under-recovers long before acoustic corruption bites. We therefore report the ladder only as a sensitivity analysis of the word-boundary-corruption mechanism and explicitly retract any reading of it as an empirical cross-layer error propagation curve. Establishing that curve properly requires re-running the fixed LLM over noise-injected audio at each level, which is outside this work's measured scope.

\subsection{Acoustic Optimizations: VAD Chunking, Domain Biasing, and Post-Correction}
\label{sec:acoustic-opt}

We implemented and \emph{measured} three interventions on the same seven clips and the same
\texttt{openai/whisper-large-v3} checkpoint. All three are reported as negative results: none of
them lowers error against the gold transcripts, and an earlier draft of this work published a
ladder for them that no artefact on disk can reproduce (Table~\ref{tab:interventions}).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Energy-Based VAD Chunking (tested, harmful on this corpus):} The chunker in
\texttt{vad\_chunker.py} scores $30$\,ms frames, cuts at
$\max(10^{-4}, 2.2\times\text{the }25\text{th-percentile noise floor})$, closes gaps
$\le200$\,ms and searches for boundaries inside pauses $>0.4$\,s to emit $12$--$28$\,s windows.
It is not a learned VAD, and the delivered GUI never calls it: the app slices fixed $30$\,s
windows (\texttt{gui/pipeline.py}). Run over all seven clips it \emph{raised} macro normalised
WER from $0.0207$ to $0.0412$ in Turkish and from $0.0101$ to $0.0262$ in English (relative
$+99.6\%$ / $+160.1\%$), and it did so on every clip individually rather than only in the
aggregate. The motivation for the intervention does not survive contact with the GPU run: the
baseline contains almost no looping to remove ($8$-gram repetition rate $0.0122$ TR, $0.0000$ EN),
so segmentation buys nothing and pays for it in lost cross-window context. Mention coverage over
the closed gold list is unchanged ($87.5\%$ TR / $96.9\%$ EN).
\item \textbf{Terminology Domain Biasing (tested, harmful and leaking by construction):} Biasing
passes the first $25$ canonical entries of \texttt{data/gold\_terms.json} to
\texttt{processor.get\_prompt\_ids}, so on the two clips that own a gold list (TR-1, EN-1) the
reference terminology is injected into the decoder that is scored against that same reference;
any coverage gain is leakage, not recognition. It also broke the run: the original
\texttt{max\_new\_tokens=440} plus the prompt overflowed Whisper's $448$ decoder positions and
\texttt{generate()} raised on every clip, which is why the previously published biasing cell
could not have been measured by this code path. With the budget clamped to
$448-\text{prompt}-16$ ($188$-token prompt on TR-1, $108$ on EN-1) the prompt does two kinds of
damage. On TR-1 the decoder re-emits the glossary instead of transcribing: a $68$-word prompt
echo prefixes the transcript, ``Proje Yönetimi'' goes from $4$ to $38$ mentions, and WER rises
$0.0333 \rightarrow 0.1562$. On EN-1 the transcript is not truncated but WER rises
$0.0194 \rightarrow 0.0869$ and wall clock rises $55\,\text{s} \rightarrow 398\,\text{s}$
($7.2\times$) to buy one additional gold mention (coverage $96.9\% \rightarrow 100.0\%$, which is
the injected list coming back). The five clips without a gold list are byte-identical to the VAD
stage, which serves as the determinism control for this comparison.
\item \textbf{LLM-Based ASR Post-Correction (tested, marginal and unusable where it matters):} The
corrector (\texttt{asr\_post\_correction.py}) rewrites the stage-3 hypotheses with the local
\texttt{Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B} checkpoint over $180$-word sliding windows, and it is the second
intervention that leaks: its \texttt{get\_domain\_context} builds the system prompt from the same
\texttt{data/gold\_terms.json} the scorer reads. Measured on all seven clips it moves macro
normalised WER from $0.0822$ to $0.1339$ in Turkish (relative $+62.9\%$) and from $0.0431$ to
$0.0418$ in English (relative $-2.9\%$). The aggregate hides a split: the correction lowers WER on
four clips (TR-3 $0.0552\rightarrow0.0424$, EN-2 $0.0318\rightarrow0.0310$, EN-3
$0.0192\rightarrow0.0183$, EN-4 $0.0346\rightarrow0.0311$), leaves EN-1 unchanged to four decimals,
nudges TR-2 up, and on TR-1 --- the clip whose glossary the prompt is built from --- it nearly
doubles the error again ($0.1562\rightarrow0.3236$) while the $8$-gram repetition rate goes
$0.1361\rightarrow0.2723$: given an already degenerate transcript the model repeats the corruption
rather than repairing it. No clip finishes the ladder below its own raw baseline
(Table~\ref{tab:interventions_perclip}). The previously published cell for this stage
($0.014$~TR / $0.007$~EN, Term $F_1 = 80.5\%$) is withdrawn, and so is its supporting stress test:
the ``$26.3\%$ simulated WER, $+29.8\%$ absolute $F_1$'' pair reproduces exactly under
\texttt{asr\_post\_correction.py --test}, where the ``corrected'' string is the gold reference
pasted verbatim and the corrected WER is therefore $0.0\%$ by construction. That is a fixture
self-check, not a measurement of noise robustness.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Automated LLM-as-a-Judge: Usage and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:llm-judge}

Scoring every explanation with two human raters does not scale, so we implemented an automated LLM-as-a-Judge that applies the same binary rubric as the annotation protocol: $R$ asks whether the extracted term is consistent with the transcript context, $D$ asks whether the explanation is correct relative to the reference definition. The judge is \texttt{Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B} run locally with \texttt{use\_mock=False}, i.e.\ real closed-form rubric calls rather than the deterministic keyword heuristic that produced \texttt{labels\_ablation\_extended.csv}.

The judge's per-item output is on disk for exactly two groups of results, and both of them name it as the rating source: the three 2026 ASR checkpoints (\texttt{labels\_ablation\_real\_judge.csv}, $200$ pairs, Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) and the two EVREN models (\texttt{labels\_evren\_judge.csv}, $374$ pairs, Table~\ref{tab:sem}). The six Whisper cells are instead scored by human annotator~1 (\texttt{labels\_a1.csv}), which is the stronger of the two sources and is kept visually separate in the tables.

\textbf{What we cannot claim.} An earlier version of this work reported a judge-versus-human validation study ($97.00\%$ agreement on $R$, $97.42\%$ on $D$, $\kappa = 0.735$, PABAK $0.940$) against the $233$ annotations of \texttt{labels\_a1.csv}. Those predictions were never written to disk, so the comparison cannot be recomputed and we withdraw it. Table~\ref{tab:llm_judge} reports instead the agreement that the stored sheets do support --- human annotator~1 against annotator~2 on the $115$ double-labelled pairs --- giving $P_o = 0.965$, $P_e = 0.841$, $\kappa_R = 0.782$, $\mathrm{PABAK}_R = 0.930$ for context consistency and perfect agreement on definition accuracy ($P_o = 1.000$, $\kappa_D = 1.000$ over $P_e = 0.949$). The $D$ row is the prevalence paradox in its clearest form: with $\approx97\%$ of items positive, chance agreement alone is $0.949$, so a high raw rate carries little information and PABAK is reported alongside $\kappa$ rather than instead of it.

Two consequences follow. First, judge-derived $R1\%$/$D1\%$ values (including the $100\%$ cells for EVREN Qwen 3.8 Flash) inherit an unquantified leniency, because no stored artifact measures this judge's false-positive rate against a human; they should be read as upper bounds on precision. Second, closing that gap is a concrete next step: re-run the judge over the $233$ already human-rated pairs, persist its per-item labels, and report $\kappa$ with a bootstrap interval.

\subsection{Hardware Resource Profiling and Deployment Pairings}
\label{sec:deployment}

Resource profiling on consumer laptop hardware (8\,GB RTX 5060, Table~\ref{tab:llmlat}) separates the two local checkpoints by a wide margin: \texttt{Qwen3.5-4B} in 4-bit completes the same clip calls in a $39.55$\,s mean on the Turkish Large-v3 feed, whereas \texttt{Qwen3.5-9B} spends $513.19$\,s and is the configuration whose weights do not stay resident, since its per-clip spread ($322.29$--$704.08$\,s over two clips) is dominated by host-memory offloading. Peak occupancy is instrumented for one checkpoint only: the 4-bit 4B probe (\texttt{outputs/local\_vram\_probe.json}) records $3.08$\,GB of weights resident, $4.72$\,GB peak allocator and $5.26$\,GB device occupancy mid-call on the $8.12$\,GB card. For every other checkpoint we report latency evidence only and claim no memory figure. We formulate three distinct deployment paradigms:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Accuracy-Oriented (Server / Batch):} Whisper Large-v3 paired with GPT-4.1 or \texttt{Qwen3.5-9B} run asynchronously, maximising terminology recall ($46.9\%$) and ASR accuracy ($0.021$ TR / $0.010$ EN). Our $9$B configuration was offloaded to host memory and took $513.19$\,s per clip mean, which is why this pairing is a batch recommendation.
\item \textbf{Practical Local Deployment (Laptop / Edge):} Whisper Large-v3 Turbo paired with \textbf{Qwen3.5-4B}, providing a completely private, zero-cost, local pipeline operating at RTF $\approx 0.03$ with $98.0\%$ English context precision. This pairing completed every run on the 8\,GB RTX~5060 host described above, and for the 4B checkpoint the mid-call device occupancy we measured ($5.26$\,GB of $8.12$\,GB) explains why; it is an observed fact about our machine rather than a sized GPU requirement, since no other checkpoint's occupancy was instrumented.
\item \textbf{Sovereign Cloud Low-Latency Paradigm (EVREN National Platform~\cite{evren2026}):} Whisper Turbo or Large-v3 for local acoustic transcription paired with \textbf{EVREN Qwen 3.8 Flash} hosted on Türkiye's national H200 GPU infrastructure. This is the configuration the latency measurements support: $6.11$\,s Turkish / $4.92$\,s English mean per-request latency, $6.5\times$ faster than the local 4-bit run on the same clips, with no semantic-layer weights resident in client VRAM. Two caveats travel with it. The ASR layer still executes locally, so client resources are reduced rather than eliminated; and routing student transcripts to a domestic operator is a data-residency posture whose KVKK status depends on the operator's processing and retention terms, which were not obtained, so no compliance claim is made. \textbf{EVREN DeepSeek V4.1 Flash} extracts substantially more terms ($107$ TR / $142$ EN against $53$ / $72$) but at $27.68$\,s / $19.44$\,s---slower than both commercial APIs---so it is a coverage option, not a latency option.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Delivery Layer: On-Demand Explainer Video Generation}
\label{sec:delivery}

The third layer is reported as an implemented and demonstrated prototype, not a scored benchmark. Seven explainer videos were generated end to end through the GUI (\texttt{gui/static/generated\_videos/}, 4 Oct 2026): $22.34$\,s ($\times$2), $26.02$\,s ($\times$2), $31.03$\,s, and $41.83$\,s ($\times$2), totalling $\approx$211\,s of animation at $854\times480$, 15\,fps, H.264 video with AAC audio, file sizes $417$\,kB--$818$\,kB. Each request traverses the full chain (context window $\to$ Qwen3.5-4B script $\to$ Manim render $\to$ Edge-TTS narration $\to$ FFmpeg mux); by construction the renderer only executes code that passes the AST allow-list, and up to two automatic repair attempts precede a guaranteed-template fallback, so a request degrades to a generic animation rather than failing outright.

Two limitations bound what this layer can claim. First, no instrumentation persists per-request timings or the fallback path taken, so generation latency and the script-quality distribution cannot be recovered post hoc; the \texttt{manim} and \texttt{edge-tts} dependencies are additionally absent from \texttt{experiments/requirements.txt}, so the layer is not reproducible from the locked environment alone. Second, the Figure Walkthrough mode (PDF pages $\to$ narrated slide video) is implemented but produced no artifacts, and the learner-profile remediation entries on disk are test fixtures rather than real usage. We therefore claim functional feasibility only, and defer perceptual quality evaluation (for instance a learner study on whether regenerated animations improve comprehension) to future work.

\section{Limitations}

This benchmark establishes rigorous experimental boundaries: (i)~All quantitative results rest on $7$ lectures, of which only TR-1 and EN-1 carry exhaustive closed-list gold terminology ($N=32$ each); the curated $32$-lecture extension is metadata and caption text without audio on disk, so it contributes coverage plans rather than evidence; (ii)~The controlled-WER ladder is an analytical model of word-boundary corruption, not a measured ASR$\to$LLM transfer, and real classroom acoustics add non-stationary noise, reverberation, and overlapping speakers that warrant in-situ validation; (iii)~Automated LLM judges require periodic recalibration against human annotations when deployed across new curricular domains; and (iv)~Local execution of larger $\ge$9B parameter models remains constrained on 8\,GB consumer GPUs; (v)~the three acoustic interventions of Sec.~\ref{sec:acoustic-opt} were each run once per clip on a single host and a single checkpoint, so their negative results bind to \emph{this} energy-VAD implementation, \emph{this} glossary length and \emph{this} decoder-position budget --- they are evidence against the specific shipped configurations, not proof that activity-based segmentation or terminology conditioning can never help.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

We presented a three-layer evaluation---speech recognition, semantic extraction, and on-demand animation delivery---of ASR and LLM components for educational speech accessibility in Turkish and English. Our measured findings are: (1)~Whisper Large-v3 gives the most accurate transcription ($0.021$ TR / $0.010$ EN normalised WER on the gold set), and Turbo is the efficient edge alternative at $0.037$ TR / $0.011$ EN, which we measure as $3.4\times$ (Turkish, $110.6$\,s $\rightarrow$ $32.5$\,s) and $7.7\times$ (English, $170.7$\,s $\rightarrow$ $22.1$\,s) faster pooled wall-clock than Large-v3 on the same host; (2)~across eleven \emph{measured} ASR$\to$LLM configurations, terminology $F_1$ stays in the $0.238$--$0.679$ band and is \emph{not} monotone in WER, so low transcription error alone does not guarantee concept recovery---the widely assumed smooth propagation curve is, in this work, an analytical model that over-predicts extraction quality by a wide margin; (3)~the semantic layer's ceiling is recall, not precision: on the closed 32-term gold lists, emitted-term precision stays high while recall remains below $0.60$; and (4)~the delivery layer is functional---$7$ explainer videos rendered end to end---but unbenchmarked.

Future work will close the gaps this audit exposed: measure the propagation curve properly by re-running the fixed LLM over noise-injected audio at each WER level, acquire the audio for the curated $32$-lecture extension before claiming its coverage, instrument the delivery layer to log latency and fallback paths, recalibrate the LLM judge against human annotations in both languages, and evaluate perceptual quality of generated animations with learners rather than asserting it.

\section{Annotation, Agreement, and Prevalence Analysis}
\label{app:iaa}

The double-annotated subset ($115$ of the $233$ pairs in annotator~1's sheet, across TR and EN) was labelled independently for context consistency ($R$) and definition accuracy ($D$).
